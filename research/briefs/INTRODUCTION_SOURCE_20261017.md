When Is a Short Demand History Enough? Decision-Sensitive Policy Learning for Inventory Control

1. Introduction

A replenishment decision cannot always wait for a long demand history. For a newly introduced item or a location with little recorded demand, the decision maker must commit inventory while the demand model is still uncertain. In a lost-sales system, an inadequate order can forfeit sales, whereas excessive replenishment incurs holding costs; positive lead times also transmit the consequences of current orders into future inventory states. These features make replenishment a sequential decision problem rather than a sequence of independent forecasts (Bijvank & Vis, 2011). The practical question is therefore not only how accurately demand can be estimated, but when the available evidence is sufficient to justify changing the replenishment policy.

Research in inventory management provides reasons to distinguish predictive accuracy from decision quality. Prak et al. (2017) show that parameter-estimation uncertainty can induce dependence between forecast errors across the lead time, with consequences for safety-stock calculations even when demand itself is not serially correlated. Using M5 data, Theodorou et al. (2025) examine how the relationship between forecast accuracy and inventory performance depends on the cost structure and replenishment setting. These findings motivate a decision-oriented interpretation of data sufficiency: different demand models may support similar replenishment decisions, while apparently modest predictive disagreements can matter when they change the balance between shortage and holding costs.

This distinction is particularly important when a learned demand model supplies the environment for policy optimization. Once fitted to a short history, such a model can generate many simulated trajectories, allowing extensive policy training. However, additional simulation reduces computational sampling error without supplying new observations of the real demand process. A policy can consequently become well optimized for an inaccurate model. Model-based reinforcement learning has long recognized this problem: PILCO explicitly propagates uncertainty in a learned probabilistic dynamics model to address model bias and improve data efficiency (Deisenroth & Rasmussen, 2011). In inventory control, the corresponding challenge is to use limited real observations without treating plentiful model-generated experience as equally informative evidence.

Recent inventory-learning research makes this a demanding methodological problem. Boute et al. (2022) emphasize the importance of inventory structure and algorithm-design choices in deep reinforcement learning. Dehaybe et al. (2024) develop forecast-conditioned policies for nonstationary demand, while Deep Controlled Learning combines approximate policy iteration with sequential halving and common random numbers to improve simulation-based action evaluation (Temizöz et al., 2025). These advances establish strong alternatives to generic reinforcement learning and clarify that reliable computation within an inventory model is a substantive research area. They also provide a starting point for studying a different uncertainty: whether a model estimated from a short demand history supports the policy improvements found within it.

Unknown demand and limited data are not unaddressed in this literature. Train, then Estimate and Decide combines broadly trained policies with online parameter estimation and explicitly studies initially unknown inventory parameters, including settings with limited and censored observations (Temizöz et al., 2026). Alvo et al. (2025), in a preprint, investigate differentiable policy optimization and structure-informed representations, including their data requirements. Accordingly, neither learning inventory policies from short histories nor incorporating known system dynamics establishes novelty. The remaining question pursued here concerns the reliability of a specific policy change: can inventory structure help determine whether its apparent economic benefit survives the demand-model uncertainty left by limited observations?

Gaussian processes (GPs) offer a useful representation for this uncertainty because their covariance structure expresses assumptions about related inputs and their posterior represents uncertainty about latent functions (Rasmussen & Williams, 2006). Within operations research, Price et al. (2019) develop a GP approach to demand unconstraining, demonstrating a role for probabilistic modeling of incompletely observed demand. That contribution concerns a distinct observation problem and does not remove the need to specify observability in inventory control. Nor does a GP automatically provide accurate uncertainty assessments from every small dataset: its usefulness depends on its prior, likelihood, and approximation. Here, the GP is a demand-model component, not a substitute for known inventory balance equations.

The treatment of model uncertainty must also be distinguished from established risk-sensitive and safe-improvement methods. Bayesian Risk Markov Decision Processes apply risk functionals to posterior parameter uncertainty and include an inventory application (Lin et al., 2022). Petrik et al. (2016) directly optimize robust regret relative to a baseline policy, recognizing that uncertainty shared by two policies can have less influence on their performance difference than on their separate values. Baseline bootstrapping likewise restricts policy changes where evidence is insufficient (Laroche et al., 2019). These studies rule out baseline-relative uncertainty assessment as a general novelty claim. Our proposed distinction lies instead in exploiting inventory-specific sensitivity to demand uncertainty when evaluating and learning policy improvements.

This study asks: when only a short demand history is available, can an inventory-informed assessment of uncertainty in policy-cost differences support better replenishment decisions than standard posterior-based policy learning, and under what conditions can those decisions approach the performance of policies trained on substantially longer histories? The hypothesis is conditional. Limited data may suffice when plausible demand models agree on economically relevant actions, but not when their disagreement concerns demand outcomes that change costly inventory commitments. The objective is to identify and exploit this distinction, rather than to establish that small datasets can universally replace large ones.

We consider a single-item, periodic-review lost-sales system with a known positive lead time and specified costs. Demand is exogenous and observed after each period; inference from censored sales is outside the core model. The scarce resource is the initial history of real demand, not simulated transitions or training updates. The primary evaluation fixes fitted model and policy parameters while actions respond to evolving inventory states and permitted information. Online model updating is a separate setting in which new observations must be counted consistently across methods.

We propose a decision-sensitive policy-learning framework that retains known stock-flow dynamics and uses a GP posterior to represent uncertainty about demand. Candidate policies and an adaptive inventory baseline are evaluated under the same sampled demand models and paired demand trajectories. The resulting object is a distribution of differences in expected operating cost, separating uncertainty about the demand model from variability within that model. Neural policy improvement is then informed by whether the estimated benefit of changing the policy remains credible across plausible models. Paired simulation and posterior sampling are established ingredients; the mechanism to be developed uses inventory sensitivity to decide which uncertain comparisons warrant caution.

The analytical objective is to connect demand-model perturbations to policy-cost differences through current availability, committed replenishments, and subsequent stock-flow evolution. A generic uncertainty penalty can discourage beneficial changes if it treats all predictive error as equally consequential. Conversely, a narrowly estimated improvement can be misleading if it ignores the propagation of an ordering decision beyond its delivery date. We therefore seek an inventory-specific error characterization that is informative enough to guide policy improvement, rather than simply attaching a general robust bound to an inventory application. Any posterior credibility statement remains conditional on its modeling assumptions; it is not a distribution-free guarantee of improved performance.

The proposed contribution has three components. First, we seek an inventory-specific characterization of how demand uncertainty affects the reliability of policy comparisons. Second, we propose a GP-informed neural policy-improvement mechanism that uses this characterization to limit unsupported changes without uniformly conservative stocking. Third, we investigate when additional demand history materially improves decisions, using operating cost rather than predictive accuracy alone to assess data sufficiency. Together, these objectives link probabilistic modeling to the evidence required for economically justified policy changes.

The evaluation will separate demand-model quality, policy optimization, and information advantages. Tractable synthetic instances will provide known-model reference solutions and controlled misspecification tests. Comparisons will include tuned inventory policies, GP-based predictive optimization, posterior-sampling policy learning without the proposed mechanism, and specialized inventory-learning methods. Crossed model-controller comparisons will isolate the mechanism's incremental value from improvements attributable to forecasting, historical information, or computation.

Historical evaluation will use M5 retail sales, with the published competition providing a documented data source (Makridakis et al., 2022) and its subsequent inventory analysis providing a directly relevant precedent (Theodorou et al., 2025). Corporación Favorita sales will support an external comparison, following their use in the inventory-learning experiments of Alvo et al. (2025). In both cases, recorded sales serve as exogenous requests in a replenishment simulator. Simulated shortages, inventory costs, and policy outcomes are not observed retailer outcomes, and these datasets do not establish access to uncensored demand. Historical replay will therefore complement, rather than replace, synthetic experiments with controlled observability.

Data budgets will distinguish target-item histories, external information, simulation, and computation. Chronological splits and matched test origins will support comparisons of cost, baseline deterioration, service outcomes, and computational requirements. Independent training replications and interval estimates will address the evaluation concerns raised by Agarwal et al. (2021); historical-replay analysis will additionally account for temporal and cross-item dependence. Claims of proximity to rich-data performance will require a prespecified non-inferiority margin, not an absence of statistically significant differences.

The subsequent sections will review related research, specify the inventory model and information assumptions, develop the analysis and learning procedure, and present the computational study. The concluding discussion will address the operational implications and limits of learning from short demand histories.

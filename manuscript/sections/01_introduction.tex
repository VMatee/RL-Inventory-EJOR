\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A replenishment decision cannot always wait for a long demand history. A retailer introducing an item or operating at a location with little recorded demand must commit stock before the demand process is well understood. In a lost-sales system, insufficient availability forfeits sales, whereas excessive replenishment incurs holding costs. Positive lead times extend these consequences: an order changes the supply pipeline before it changes the stock available to customers. Replenishment is therefore a sequential decision problem in which both uncertainty and earlier commitments matter \citep{bijvank2011}. The operational question is not simply whether a more flexible policy can be trained, but whether the available evidence justifies replacing an established replenishment rule.

Inventory research provides reasons to distinguish the accuracy of demand estimates from their decision value. \citet{prak2017} show that parameter-estimation uncertainty can induce dependence between forecast errors across the lead time, affecting safety-stock calculations even when demand itself is not serially correlated. Using M5 data, \citet{theodorou2025} examine how the relationship between forecast accuracy and inventory performance depends on product characteristics, replenishment settings, and costs. These findings motivate a decision-oriented interpretation of data sufficiency. Demand models that disagree predictively may still favor similar orders, whereas a smaller disagreement can be consequential when it changes a costly stocking decision. More history is valuable to the extent that it resolves uncertainty that matters for decisions.

The distinction becomes especially important when an estimated demand model supplies the environment for reinforcement learning. A short history can support an arbitrarily large collection of simulated trajectories, but additional simulation does not provide new observations of the underlying demand process. It can improve optimization within the estimated model while leaving uncertainty about that model unresolved. A policy may consequently exploit an estimation error rather than a genuine replenishment opportunity. This difficulty is not new to model-based learning: PILCO propagates uncertainty in a probabilistic dynamics model to address model bias and improve data efficiency \citep{deisenroth2011}. For inventory control, the challenge is to distinguish scarce real observations from abundant simulated experience and use the former to assess whether improvements found with the latter are credible.

Recent inventory-learning research establishes a demanding basis for this assessment. \citet{boute2022} emphasize inventory structure and algorithm-design choices in deep reinforcement learning. \citet{dehaybe2024} develop forecast-conditioned policies for nonstationary demand. Deep Controlled Learning uses approximate policy iteration, sequential halving, and common random numbers to improve simulation-based action evaluation \citep{temizoz2025}. Train, then Estimate and Decide combines general-policy training with online parameter estimation and explicitly considers initially unknown parameters and limited or censored observations \citep{temizoz2026}. In a preprint, \citet{alvo2025} investigate differentiable policy optimization and structure-informed representations, including their data requirements. Together, these studies establish that inventory learning, model-informed simulation, and learning with limited information are substantive existing capabilities. Merely combining them does not identify the contribution of a new method.

Gaussian processes (GPs) provide a representation of demand-model uncertainty: covariance assumptions connect related inputs, while posterior inference represents uncertainty about latent functions \citep{rasmussen2006}. Their role in operations research extends beyond generic prediction. For example, \citet{price2019} develop GP-based demand unconstraining under an explicit observation model. Here, however, the GP represents the unknown demand process rather than learning inventory balance equations that are already known. Its usefulness with short histories depends on the prior, likelihood, and approximation; neither small-data superiority nor calibrated uncertainty is automatic. Random demand conditional on a model must also be separated from uncertainty about which model applies. Otherwise, a controller may mistake ordinary demand variability for a lack of evidence, or overlook substantial model uncertainty behind a precise simulation estimate.

There is equally important prior work on deciding whether a learned policy improves on a baseline. Bayesian Risk Markov Decision Processes apply risk functionals to posterior parameter uncertainty and include an inventory application \citep{lin2022}. \citet{petrik2016} directly minimize robust baseline regret, assessing both policies under the same uncertain model rather than comparing separately constructed extremes. Baseline bootstrapping constrains policy changes when the available evidence is insufficient \citep{laroche2019}. Baseline-relative uncertainty is therefore an established principle, not our claimed innovation. The question pursued here is whether the known stock-flow structure of a lost-sales system can make that principle more informative and computationally useful when uncertainty originates in a short demand history.

We ask: \emph{When is a limited demand history sufficient to justify a learned replenishment policy, and can inventory-specific sensitivity to demand uncertainty improve that decision relative to generic posterior-based policy learning?} A related empirical question is how much initial history is needed to approach the performance of a policy trained on substantially more pre-deployment data. The hypothesis is conditional: short histories may suffice when plausible demand models support similar economically relevant actions, but not when unresolved disagreement changes costly inventory commitments. We do not presume that small datasets can universally substitute for large ones.

We consider a single-item, periodic-review lost-sales system with a known, fixed positive lead time and specified operating costs. Demand is exogenous and observed after each period; inference from censored sales is outside the core model. The scarce resource is the real demand history available before deployment, not the number of simulated transitions or optimizer updates. In the primary setting, fitted demand-model and policy parameters remain fixed during evaluation, while replenishment actions respond to inventory states and permitted information. Online parameter updating is a separate comparison with consistently counted additional observations. These choices isolate the effect of initial data scarcity without conflating it with active demand discovery or repeated retraining.

The proposed framework compares a candidate policy $\pi$ with a tuned baseline $\pi_b$ through
\begin{equation}
\Delta_P(\pi,\pi_b)=J_P(\pi)-J_P(\pi_b),
\label{eq:intro-policy-difference}
\end{equation}
where $P$ denotes a demand process and $J_P$ is expected operating cost under a common evaluation horizon and initial-state specification. Negative values favor the candidate. GP posterior models and paired demand trajectories provide a computational basis for evaluating this difference. Demand variability is averaged within each model, while variation across models represents epistemic uncertainty; Monte Carlo error is assessed separately. The baseline is a transparent comparator, not an assumption of optimality or guaranteed safety. The intended role of uncertainty is to inform policy improvement, rather than merely to append a predictive variance to the policy input.

The analytical objective is to relate demand-model perturbations to changes in policy-cost differences through available stock, committed arrivals, and subsequent replenishment decisions. The same demand perturbation can have different economic effects depending on these quantities. A uniform uncertainty penalty may therefore discourage useful changes, while an optimistic comparison may conceal future shortages or excess stock. Importantly, an order's influence need not end on its delivery date: residual inventory and later decisions can transmit its effects further. We seek an inventory-specific characterization that respects this propagation and is useful for learning, rather than a generic robustness statement relabeled for an inventory application. Any posterior credibility statement remains model-conditional, and adaptively selected policies require appropriate treatment before a stronger coverage claim is justified.

The intended contribution has three connected components. First, the study seeks a structural characterization of the demand uncertainty relevant to replenishment-policy comparisons. Second, it proposes GP-informed neural policy improvement that uses this characterization to limit unsupported changes without uniformly conservative stocking. Third, it examines data sufficiency through operating cost and the consequences of harmful policy changes, identifying conditions under which additional history does or does not alter economically sensible decisions. The methodological claim depends on incremental value beyond ordinary posterior-sampling policy learning and generic baseline-relative methods; combining GP modeling with reinforcement learning alone is insufficient.

The computational study will separate demand estimation, policy optimization, and uncertainty treatment. Tractable synthetic instances will supply known-model reference solutions and controlled misspecification tests. Comparisons will include tuned inventory rules, simple Bayesian demand models, GP-based predictive optimization, ordinary GP-based policy learning, and specialized inventory-learning methods. A critical ablation will hold the GP, policy optimizer, and information budget fixed while changing only the decision-sensitive mechanism. Replacing the GP with an alternative uncertainty model will test whether any benefit is specific to its representation. Real-data budgets, simulated transitions, and computational time will be reported separately; more expensive optimization must not masquerade as better use of limited observations.

Historical evaluation will use M5 retail sales \citep{makridakis2022}, whose inventory implications have been studied directly by \citet{theodorou2025}. Corporaci\'{o}n Favorita sales will provide an external comparison following their use by \citet{alvo2025}. These are sales-driven inventory simulations, not evaluations of observed retailer replenishment policies. Recorded sales will be treated as exogenous requests; simulated shortages and costs will not be presented as measured retailer outcomes or evidence of uncensored demand. Data access, preprocessing, and chronological splits must be documented before these evaluations are conducted.

All methods will use matched test origins, with validation, calibration, and pretraining information included in the data accounting. Longer-history comparators will receive only observations available before the same deployment cutoff. Because older data may also reflect a different regime, controlled experiments will distinguish data sufficiency from recency effects. Cost--data frontiers, baseline deterioration, service outcomes, and runtime will be accompanied by uncertainty estimates, motivated by the evaluation concerns of \citet{agarwal2021}. Historical comparisons will additionally account for temporal and cross-item dependence. Proximity to rich-data performance will require a prespecified non-inferiority margin, rather than an absence of statistically significant differences.

The subsequent sections will review related research, formalize the inventory model and information assumptions, develop the analysis and policy-learning procedure, and present the computational study. The concluding discussion will examine when limited demand histories support economically justified policy changes and where additional information remains necessary.
